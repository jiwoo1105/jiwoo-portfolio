Context & my role

From visual observations to inspectable measurements.

Comparing dribbling technique requires a clear account of what was measured and over which frames. This project uses body landmarks and ball trajectories from monocular video to quantify upper-body movement and show the footage behind each measurement.

I defined the evaluation goals and metrics, directed comparative experiments, and reviewed the source footage. Working with AI coding tools, I developed and checked the pipeline from video input to metric calculation and HTML/JSON reporting.

Challenge & approach

Tracking the wrong ball also changes when technique is measured.

The default pipeline extracts 33 landmarks in image coordinates and estimated 3D coordinates with MediaPipe Pose Heavy. YOLOv8n initializes the ball location, SAM2.1 tracks its mask, and the horizontal trajectory and proximity to the ankles provide candidate touch events.

I found cases where SAM2 followed a background ball instead of the player’s ball. I selected the initial target by ankle proximity and, in that experiment, triggered re-detection when the ball-to-ankle distance exceeded 250 pixels for five consecutive frames. I then compared the same frames with YOLO11s re-detection in an ankle-centered ROI to check that the target was the actual dribbling ball.

The 250-pixel threshold depends on the input video conditions. This demo visualizes actual detections from the separate YOLO11 experiment, without interpolating missed detections or claiming a dataset-wide tracking improvement.

Metric design

Measuring shoulder motion within 16 frames of each touch.

I smooth the 3D unit vector along the shoulder line and multiply the angular change between adjacent frames by FPS to obtain angular speed. The current method averages the angular speeds computed from valid adjacent-frame pairs within 16 frames before and after each touch, counting overlapping pairs only once.

The head-up metric measures the angular range of the shoulder-midpoint–eye-midpoint vector relative to the vertical axis around touches. The torso metric averages knee–hip–shoulder angles across all valid frames. Each metric states its measurement and time window explicitly.

Results & validation

Checking the calculation and the meaning of the score.

Using saved inference outputs from three videos totaling 771 frames, I regenerated the ±16-frame reports and checked that the shoulder aggregation and total score agreed with an independent calculation within 1e−10. The total is marked N/A when a required metric cannot be calculated.

This verifies computational consistency. The available expert grades are not ground-truth scores for each metric, and independent validation of skill assessment remains open. Occlusion, monocular depth errors, and false touch detections can still affect the measurements.

As of 13 October 2026. The demo illustrates tracking; it does not establish the accuracy of automated skill scoring.

What I learned

Detecting a ball and selecting the right ball are different tasks.

A wrong target propagates into touch timing and pose metrics. I learned to inspect target selection, candidate touches, and measurement windows separately against the source frames, rather than checking only the final score. Reproducible calculations and valid assessment metrics also require separate evidence.
