01 · The problem

More movement did not necessarily mean better dribbling.

Beyond estimating pose from a monocular video, the harder question was what measurement could represent the evaluation intent. Maximum shoulder-direction differences were also large in lower-rated videos. Changing score weights or caps did not change the ordering of the raw measurements.

I translated faculty feedback into calculation definitions and built an analysis flow that exposes source footage, joints, ball trajectories and metrics together. The project concluded in June 2026; the September–November comparisons and reruns shown here are subsequent research reviews.

02 · Redefining the measurement

Separating movement magnitude from speed, then defining when to measure.

I compared maximum-angle candidates separately from average directional angular speed and examined ±6, 10, 16, 20, 24 and 30-frame windows. Following faculty feedback, I retained the framewise speed calculation but averaged only the values around touches.

The final ±16 window aligns the interval with the head-up metric. Overlapping frame pairs are counted once, missing intervals are not bridged, and an unavailable required metric leaves the total score as N/A.

What I verified

The measurement, interval and missing-data rules became explicit. The comparisons did not establish consistent improvement in rating separation or an optimal window.

What changed in my approach

I stopped treating an overall expert rating as ground truth for one joint metric. Before fitting a score, I needed to explain the movement the underlying number actually measured.

03 · Tracing input errors

Choosing the wrong ball also changes the moments being evaluated.

The default path combines MediaPipe pose estimates with YOLOv8n initialization and SAM2.1 tracking. Selecting a background ball affects touch candidates and measurement windows, so I overlaid the tracked coordinates on the source footage.

I reviewed ankle-proximity selection, reacquisition and a separate YOLO11 ankle-ROI redetection experiment. A background ball could still satisfy the proximity criterion, so the problem was not fully resolved. The outputs make input-level failures visible even when a final score alone would hide them.

What changed in my approach

A detection existing, the correct object being selected, and the resulting metric being valid are three different questions. I learned to review them separately.

04 · Follow-up validation

A repeatable result can also be a repeatable error.

For a later paper review, I reprocessed three source videos five times each with the same CLI and CPU settings. Joint arrays, ball trajectories, touch frames, raw metrics and scores matched across repeats. Separately, recomputing 771 saved frames agreed with an independent calculation within 1e−10.

The November review also identified incorrect initial ball selection. I therefore recorded repeatability separately from measurement accuracy and kept source-level object checks as a necessary validation step.

05 · What I learned

Revisiting the definition before adjusting the number.

I separated the measurement, its time interval and input quality before adjusting weights. When results differed from expectations, I learned to investigate definition → input → calculation → interpretation instead of changing the final score alone.
