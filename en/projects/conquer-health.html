Context & my role

Making retrieved documents useful to the answer.

The task was to improve medical question answering without changing the provided L2-preview weights. A successful lookup was insufficient if the relevant document text did not reach generation or the answer missed part of the question.

I implemented the initial MCP retrieval/generation connection and medication safety gate, checking evidence delivery separately from whether the response addressed the question and used that evidence. I also worked on output-budget experiments and multi-turn response instructions. Individual contributions and the final team submission are distinguished by commit.

My implementation

I checked the gap between successful retrieval and an evidence-informed answer.

I connected document lookup with delivery of the retrieved body text into generation through a Python/FastAPI MCP flow. Beyond checking tool returns, I examined missing or duplicate results, the text actually passed to the model, unsupported citations, and unanswered requirements.

My experiment

A larger output budget did not establish a score improvement.

After rechecking the output limit, my candidate e6de327 corrected the configured server cap from 2,048 to 32,768 and raised the requested output budget from 2,048 to 4,096 tokens. The goal was to reduce truncation that triggered regeneration. The server cap and requested output budget are distinct settings.

The ledger records 49.59 for this candidate, 0.41 points above the baseline’s three-run mean of 49.18. The difference lies within the observed run-to-run variation, so it does not establish a benefit from increasing the token budget alone. Truncation, fallback frequency, and latency also needed inspection.

Team decisions & scope

We reserved tools for questions that needed them.

After establishing a direct-generation baseline, the team explored selective MCP use for precise lookups such as Korean drug prices, approvals, ingredients, and disease codes. Direct generation remained the fallback for failed lookups or ambiguous targets, and post-processing was adjusted to preserve long answers.

This describes the team’s final submission family. My initial integration, evidence delivery, safety gate, and output-budget experiment are identified by their commits. Without separate call logs, I do not attribute a specific share of the final score to selective MCP.

Results & award

First in the benchmark—with experimental and final scores kept separate.

Team AIM won the benchmark award, ranking first among 14 teams in the final HealthBench-based evaluation at Lunit Conquer Health. The three-round mean was 46.51. This is a shared team outcome, not an effect attributable solely to MCP or output budget.

Why the development dashboard shows other scores

The development ledger’s 54.37 and the closing dashboard’s 53.53 are separate from the three final rounds. Some development runs also differed in server state and accumulated changes, so these records are not combined into one improvement rate. The certificate documents the award, not numerical scores.

What I learned

A change I can verify matters more than a promising score.

Repeated evaluations of the same behavior ranged from 47.79 to 50.03—a 2.24-point spread. My output-budget candidate’s 0.41-point difference from the baseline mean fell within that variation, so I did not claim an improvement. I learned to examine truncation, retries, latency, and completeness alongside the score.

What mattered was whether the information reached the answer and whether a change held up across evaluations. I now keep configuration, observations, and interpretation separate in experiment records.
