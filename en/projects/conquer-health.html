Project overview

A medical AI hackathon focused on the response workflow around a provided model.

The hackathon asked us to improve medical question answering without changing Lunit’s L2-preview weights. Adding retrieval tools or a larger output budget did not by itself ensure that answers addressed the question completely and used the evidence.

I implemented the initial MCP lookup, evidence delivery and medication safety gate, and experimented with output budgets and multi-turn instructions. I separated lookup, model input, generation and post-processing to inspect where information could be lost.

Challenge 1 · Delivering evidence to generation

How does retrieved evidence reach the final answer?

The challenge
With model weights fixed, improvements had to come from retrieval, input construction and the response flow. Stopping at a document list or returning an empty selection after collection could leave generation without the retrieved evidence.
Why this approach
I inspected the actual text crossing from retrieval to generation and added recovery for list-only termination and empty selections. My implementation scope was the initial MCP integration and evidence delivery.

What I did

The retrieval model could stop after listing documents or return an empty final selection despite having collected evidence. I added a prompt to fetch the document body when it stopped at exploration, and a recovery path that uses the collected evidence when the final selection is empty.

After establishing a direct-generation baseline, the team explored limiting tools to questions needing lookup, such as Korean pricing, approvals, ingredients and disease codes. My contribution was the initial integration and evidence delivery; selective lookup and preserving long answers belong to the team submission.

What I learned

What mattered was whether needed information survived into the final answer, not how many tools were used. I learned to inspect the boundaries where information could disappear.

Challenge 2 · Judging improvement

What counts as improvement when evaluation scores vary?

The challenge
Repeated evaluations of the same serving behavior ranged from 47.79 to 50.03. A higher score after changing settings to reduce truncation had to be assessed against that variability.
Why this approach
I adjusted the server limit and request output budget as separate settings and compared the candidate with repeated baseline records.

What I did

To investigate truncated answers and regeneration, candidate e6de327 corrected the server cap from 2,048 to 32,768 while raising the requested output budget from 2,048 to 4,096 tokens. I checked these as distinct settings.

The candidate scored 49.59 against a three-run baseline mean of 49.18. The +0.41 difference was within the observed baseline spread of 47.79–50.03, or 2.24 points. This was insufficient evidence to attribute an improvement to the larger token budget.

What I verified

I retained the settings and scores and identified truncation, fallback, latency and completeness as additional checks. This development experiment is separate from the final competition evaluation.

What I learned

Instead of selecting the highest single score, I first considered whether the change could be distinguished from baseline variation. I learned to record the change, observation and supported conclusion separately.

Results & learning

A team benchmark win and a clearer standard for evaluating change.

Team AIM won the benchmark award, ranking first among 14 teams in the final HealthBench-based evaluation at Lunit Conquer Health. The three-round mean was 46.51. This is a shared team outcome, not an effect attributable solely to MCP or output budget.

What I learned

Connecting MCP or generating longer answers did not alone explain an improvement. I learned to separate controllable settings, inspect actual inputs and responses, and distinguish run-to-run variation from the effects of team changes.
